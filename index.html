    AI in Science: Early Insights

    Fifteen million Gemini interactions, 2,690 specialized AI models and a survey of 637 scientists, sorted into one list of scientific tasks. Four findings on how AI is changing the way science gets done.

    Mihai Codreanu, Alex Imas, Juan Mateos-Garcia and 15 co-authors. Google, Google DeepMind and MIT FutureTech, September 2026. A visual reading of the 42-page paper.

    Three sources, one list of tasks

    Earlier studies counted papers, patents and citations, which arrive late. This paper adds three new sources and maps all of them onto the same taxonomy of scientific tasks, so chat use and specialized models can be compared directly.

    “Together, the three data sources let us follow AI from capability, to adoption, to its effect on how science gets done.”
    p. 4

    1

    Scientists use AI more than most workers, in almost every field

    Research-heavy jobs make up a far larger share of Gemini conversations than of US employment. Science use appears in 195 of 217 scientific subfields, and specialized models cover all 26 OpenAlex fields.

    “Scientists lead other occupations in the use of AI.”
    p. 3

        Use tracks the size of a country’s science workforce

        A one percentage point larger share of the world’s researchers goes with an almost 0.9 point larger share of science Gemini interactions. That simple relationship explains about three quarters of the variation between countries. The US, South Korea, Japan, Australia, Singapore and the Netherlands show high adoption; countries with smaller science workforces in Africa and Central and South Asia show less (p. 12).

        The authors caution that high citation counts may be partly built into the sample, since the search favored notable models. They read these numbers as qualitative evidence, or a lower bound (p. 20, note 22).

        General LLMs and specialized models do different jobs

        From a distance they look alike: analyzing and modeling quantitative data is the top task for both. Up close, the overlap almost disappears. The paper calls them complements.

        What each kind of model is used for

        General LLMs (Gemini logs)

        • Writing and troubleshooting research code
        • Statistical analysis and hypothesis testing
        • Literature review and synthesis
        • Drafting manuscripts and documents
        • Teaching, operations and project management

        Specialized models (inventory)

        • Predicting disease and clinical outcomes
        • Engineering molecular constructs and reagents
        • Computational molecular modeling
        • Predicting and simulating data such as protein structures, genomic modalities and weather
        • Generating, predicting and classifying, the top three actions

        Specialized models cover 17.6% of the taxonomy’s most detailed tasks. Excluding operational and teaching tasks, which abstracts rarely mention, that rises to 24.3%: almost 1 in 4 substantive research tasks. Coverage says nothing yet about how well a model does the task (pp. 16–17).

          Models travel across fields

          The paper traced 460,000 citations to the inventory’s models since 2020. Over a quarter of citation links cross a domain boundary. Models from health, life and social sciences travel furthest; physical-science models mostly stay home (p. 20).

          Where citations to each domain’s models come from

          From p. 20.

          One reading: techniques get reabsorbed into computer science. U-Net was designed for medical lesion segmentation and spread across volumetric computer vision (p. 20).

          Built in a few countries, used in many

          The top 10 developer countries and regions account for 84% of the models, led by the US, China, the UK, EU countries, Korea and Canada. Citations look different: China accounts for around 40% of them, and several lower- and middle-income countries in Latin America, South-East Asia and Africa are relatively unlikely to build models but more likely to cite them. The authors suggest openly available models may reach places that lack the resources to build them (pp. 20–22).

          Scientists report saving nearly 7 hours a week

          Just below three quarters of surveyed scientists say AI saves them time on net; 6% say it costs them time. The average saving is about 6.9 hours a week, and most of it goes back into research.

          “That time goes back into research—into more output.”
          p. 5

          How the saved time is reinvested

          From p. 23–24. The paper gives these as approximate shares.

          For context: scientists spend just under a fifth of their week on data analysis, the work most associated with AI, and slightly more on data collection and experimentation (p. 23). That is one reason task-level savings may not yet show up as more discoveries.

          Output, so far and expected

          About 84% report higher lab or professional output over the past three years. Looking ahead, about 9 in 10 expect increases, and the share expecting a rise of 25% or more jumps from about 7% to about 18%.

          The bottleneck moves downstream

          As some stages of research get easier, the constraint shifts to what AI can’t speed up yet: lab work, validation and checking AI’s own output. Scientists also report drifting toward safer questions.

          “AI tools may have increased the number of viable theories and hypotheses but not necessarily the means to test them.”
          p. 5

            More ambitious, and more cautious

            Most scientists say AI widened their reach across disciplines and raised the ambition of their questions. But on their own projects, a plurality say it pushes them toward safer, incremental work. The authors link this to a “streetlight effect”: AI makes data-rich, benchmarked problems cheaper, which can crowd out riskier questions where data is scarce and testing is costly (p. 26).

            The authors add that incremental “normal science” isn’t necessarily bad, for example work that deepens understanding of disease mechanisms (p. 30, note 28).

            Where the evidence is thin

            The authors describe this as an early snapshot. These are the limits they list.

            • Only Gemini, and not all of it

              Logs exclude enterprise and paid API use, and agentic tools such as Antigravity. Gemini users may differ from users of other models.

            • The model inventory favors the visible

              It captures notable models found through search, bibliometrics and code repositories, and misses bespoke, fine-tuned and commercial ones. Citations lag.

            • The survey may attract enthusiasts

              637 scientists in the US and UK, unweighted and not representative. Keen AI users may be more likely to answer, which could inflate adoption and time savings.

            • Tasks come from job ads

              The line between science and the wider STEM economy is hard to draw, so some ads are misclassified.

            • Classifiers can’t see who is asking

              Science is labeled only when the conversation suggests it, and each conversation or model task maps to a single task.

            • A moment in time, not a cause

              The data covers early to mid 2026, and the results are associations. The division of labor between model types may change as models improve.

            “Our results establish associations rather than causality.”
            p. 29

            What comes next

            Today a researcher moves between tools by hand: a chatbot for code and drafting, AlphaFold or a materials model for prediction, a spreadsheet or lab notebook in between. The paper sketches a next step: an LLM that orchestrates specialized models, plans the experiment, calls the right model for each step, checks outputs against each other and hands back a candidate answer. The scientist’s job shifts toward choosing the question, setting the standard of evidence and deciding what is worth a physical test.

            Getting there, the authors argue, means investing in physical experimentation and validation capacity, in verification tools, and in the data and benchmarks that would let AI take on riskier questions rather than safer ones.

            “Our data say we are not there yet: verification still consumes a large share of the time AI saves and the backlog of untested hypotheses is growing.”
            p. 31

            In 2016, AlphaGo’s 37th move against Lee Sedol was one its own model estimated a human would play about one time in ten thousand. It won. The paper’s hope is that AI in science will eventually make moves of that kind.

            Paraphrased from p. 31.
